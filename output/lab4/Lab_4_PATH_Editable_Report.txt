Testing examined PATH camera controls, object detection, saved accessibility settings, voice feedback, haptic feedback, and the work-in-progress Hazard Log. The session found working camera control, empty-scene uncertainty messaging, settings reflow, and a reversible sample log. An initial silent-voice observation was traced to scrcpy audio forwarding; after forwarding was disabled, the operator confirmed voice output. A separate desktop gallery check detected the target class in eight of eight selected images, but matched only 11 of 17 target annotations at intersection over union (IoU) of at least 0.50. This is a demonstration diagnostic, not an independent accuracy or navigation-safety result.

5 Supplementary Gallery Model Testing

The unchanged bundled FP32 TFLite model was run on the eight JPEG files in qa/phone_test_gallery. The model checksum matched its metadata (SHA-256 starts 6baef465b1366efb). Input was 512 x 512 RGB; output was 1 x 12 x 5376. The runner used app-style floor sampling, gray letterboxing, confidence greater than 0.25, same-class NMS at IoU greater than 0.50, and a maximum of 12 detections. JPEG decoding bypasses the phone camera's YUV conversion and sensor rotation.

Findings: All eight images contained a prediction localized to at least one expected target. Six target annotations were unmatched: one person, three cars and two motorcycles. Two target predictions did not match an annotation: one chair and one car. Black, gray and white synthetic images produced no detections (3/3 controls). The selected gallery favors historical successful examples, so neither 8/8 image coverage nor 11/17 matched annotations is a general accuracy estimate. Source labels, including the motorcycle fixture's toy ride-ons, also need semantic review before drawing broader conclusions.

Individual Reflection

Jan Miko Guevarra | Group 8 | Lab 4

Testing PATH showed me that an application can demonstrate a working feature while still having important reliability limits. The camera detected the chair image and displayed its class and direction. I also confirmed that both voice and vibration worked. However, the gallery run showed why I should not judge reliability from one successful demonstration: the expected class appeared in all eight selected images, but only 11 of 17 target annotations were matched.

The supplementary multi-object gallery test, particularly 01_person.jpg, revealed the most critical limitation because the model matched only three of four labeled people. Across the gallery, one person, three cars and two motorcycles were unmatched against the supplied labels. The model also produced unmatched chair and car boxes. These results need annotation review and broader testing, but they already show a limitation that matters for an assistive-navigation prototype. Finding one object is not the same as finding every relevant object. The app must continue treating a scene without detections as unknown rather than telling the user that the path is clear.

One surprising behavior was the initial lack of audible speech even though the interface displayed a last announcement. The first explanation could have been a broken voice feature. Instead, the test environment was forwarding the phone audio through scrcpy. After audio forwarding was disabled and the announcement was repeated, I could hear the voice. This taught me to record the device setup and check where audio is being played before concluding that the app itself has failed. Vibration and voice were working after the retest; the initial observation and its resolution should both remain in the report.

Testing also helped me think more carefully about accessibility. At the enlarged text size, the settings changed into a longer layout that could be scrolled. This matters because a screen can look good at its default size but become difficult to use when someone increases the text. I would still need feedback from intended users to know whether the wording, controls and spoken information are easy to understand. A layout check alone does not establish that the app is effective for mobility.

The Hazard Log demonstrated another difference between a working interface and a completed feature. Clear and Undo worked on the sample entries, but those entries were explicitly marked as preview data. I should not describe that as working live history or GPS. Clear labels help users and reviewers understand what the prototype can currently do, while giving the team a concrete next step for development.

I would continue using automated tests for settings, transforms, tracking and feedback cancellation because these checks can reveal regressions whenever the app changes. The 117 passing Flutter tests are useful evidence for those behaviors. I would combine them with phone checks for actual voice, vibration and camera interaction, and use a larger independently reviewed image set for detection evaluation. Before submitting the report, I would review the assigned roles with my group and complete the remaining cases instead of changing an incomplete result into a pass.
